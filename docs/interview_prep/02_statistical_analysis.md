# Interview Prep: Statistical Analysis Module

**1. Why Welch's t-test instead of a standard (Student's) t-test for the primary endpoint?**
Welch's t-test doesn't assume equal variance between groups, while Student's t-test does. With independently-randomized arms of different underlying response heterogeneity (as in my data, where individual responsiveness is a random draw), there's no reason to assume Drug X and Placebo have identical outcome-score variance — and testing for it isn't necessary when Welch's is strictly safer as a default. In this project it found t=18.05, p<0.001, comparing 60.19 (Drug X) vs 51.14 (Placebo).

**2. Walk me through why you ran a Shapiro-Wilk test before the t-test.**
Welch's t-test is fairly robust to moderate non-normality at this sample size (n=1,000 per arm), but I still checked normality (`normality_check` in `statistical_tests.py`) to decide whether a non-parametric alternative was warranted anywhere in the pipeline. Where it wasn't (e.g. comparing HbA1c reduction across three AE-severity groups, which had unequal, skewed group sizes: 773/1,065/162), I used Kruskal-Wallis instead of ANOVA — it found H=83.74, p<0.001, meaning HbA1c reduction genuinely differs by AE severity group (medians 0.40 / 0.67 / 0.58 for No/Mild/Severe AE).

**3. What does Cohen's d = 0.81 actually tell a non-technical stakeholder, and why report it alongside the p-value?**
The p-value only tells you whether a difference is unlikely to be due to chance — it says nothing about how big the difference is, and with a large enough sample even a trivial difference becomes "significant." Cohen's d standardizes the mean difference by the pooled standard deviation, so 0.81 says the two arms differ by about four-fifths of a standard deviation — a large, practically meaningful effect, not just a statistically detectable one. I report both together specifically so a business audience doesn't overweight the p-value alone.

**4. How did you decide between a chi-square test and Fisher's exact test for the adverse-event comparisons?**
Chi-square is appropriate when expected cell counts are reasonably large (a common rule of thumb is ≥5 per cell), which holds here — e.g. the AE-occurrence comparison has 1,000 patients per arm split across two outcome categories, giving expected counts well above that threshold (chi²=127.61, p<0.001). Fisher's exact test would be the safer choice for small or sparse contingency tables (e.g. comparing a rare severe-AE code between two very small subgroups), which is a good follow-up question to flag as a limitation of my chi-square use if the cell counts were smaller.

**5. All these tests came back significant — doesn't that undermine the demo? How would you know if your pipeline could detect a null result?**
I built and unit-tested the stats module against synthetic data with a known zero effect specifically to confirm it doesn't always say "significant" (see `tests/test_statistical_tests.py::test_welch_t_test_no_difference`, which asserts `significant is False` on two identically-distributed samples). In the actual trial data, every test being significant reflects that I deliberately generated a real, non-trivial treatment effect and AE burden — the tests are doing their job correctly on data designed to have a genuine signal, not rubber-stamping everything.
