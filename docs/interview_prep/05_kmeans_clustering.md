# Interview Prep: K-Means Patient Clustering

**1. How did you choose k=4, and why not just pick the "elbow" visually?**
I computed the elbow curve (inertia vs. k, k=2..8) for a visual sanity check, but for the actual decision I compared silhouette scores specifically between k=3 and k=4 — the two clinically-interpretable candidates the project scoped for — and k=4 won narrowly (0.089 vs 0.086). Silhouette score is a more principled tiebreaker than eyeballing an elbow chart, since the elbow here is fairly gradual rather than sharply bent (visible in `docs/screenshots/clustering_elbow_method.png`), which is common with real-world-shaped data that doesn't separate into obviously distinct blobs.

**2. Your silhouette score (0.089) is quite low — doesn't that mean the clusters are bad?**
A low silhouette score means the clusters aren't well-separated in the standardized feature space — which is true and worth saying plainly rather than hiding. But cluster quality for a business use case isn't the same question as geometric separation: even with overlapping PCA projections, the four clusters have sharply different real-world profiles (the AE-Prone cluster has a 99.2% any-AE rate and 40.8% dropout vs. ~7% dropout in the other three), which is what actually matters for a monitoring-prioritization decision. Patient phenotypes in general tend to sit on a continuum rather than in tight natural clusters, so a modest silhouette score is the expected, honest result here, not a red flag to paper over.

**3. Walk me through your feature selection and preprocessing for clustering.**
Features: age, BMI, baseline systolic/diastolic BP, baseline HbA1c, baseline LDL, baseline eGFR, primary outcome score, and each patient's worst adverse-event severity encountered (mapped None/Mild/Moderate/Severe → 0-3). I deliberately excluded treatment arm from the feature set — the goal was patient phenotypes that cut across treatment allocation, not a proxy for "which arm were you in" — though I do report arm composition per cluster during profiling. All features are standardized with `StandardScaler` before K-Means, since it's a distance-based algorithm and the raw features are on very different scales (age in years vs. outcome score 0-100).

**4. How did you turn four numbered clusters into business-meaningful labels without just eyeballing them?**
I used a ranked, rule-based approach rather than manual judgment calls (`label_all_clusters` in `patient_clustering.py`): any cluster with an elevated severe-AE rate is labeled "AE-Prone" first, since safety takes priority over efficacy in the labeling logic; among the remaining clusters, the highest mean outcome score becomes "High Responders," the lowest becomes "Non-Responders," and anything in between is "Stable/Average Responders." This is deterministic and reproducible — rerunning with different data would relabel consistently rather than requiring a human to reinterpret the clusters each time.

**5. What would you do differently if a stakeholder said "these clusters don't feel actionable"?**
I'd first check whether the objection is about label naming or about whether the underlying groups are real — the AE-Prone cluster's 100% share of severe adverse events is a real, actionable pattern regardless of what we call it. If the objection is about actionability specifically, I'd explore supervised segmentation instead (e.g. a decision tree predicting "will this patient become AE-Prone" from baseline covariates alone, before any post-baseline data exists) so the output is a rule clinicians can apply prospectively at enrollment, rather than a retrospective grouping that requires trial data to already exist.
