# Interview Prep: K-Means Patient Clustering

**1. How did you choose k=3, and why not just pick the "elbow" visually?**
I computed the elbow curve (inertia vs. k, k=2..8) for a visual sanity check, but for the actual decision I compared silhouette scores specifically between k=3 and k=4 — the two clinically-interpretable candidates the project scoped for — and k=3 won narrowly (0.082 vs 0.079). Silhouette score is a more principled tiebreaker than eyeballing an elbow chart, since the elbow here is fairly gradual rather than sharply bent (visible in `docs/screenshots/clustering_elbow_method.png`), which is common with real-world-shaped data that doesn't separate into obviously distinct blobs. When I scaled the dataset up from 500 to 2,000 patients, I reran this exact same k=3-vs-k=4 comparison rather than assuming the earlier answer still held — and the winner actually flipped (k=4 had won at the smaller sample size), which is itself a useful data point: the "right" k for a soft, continuum-shaped clustering problem can be genuinely sensitive to sample size, so I don't treat it as a fixed constant to hardcode.

**2. Your silhouette score (0.082) is quite low — doesn't that mean the clusters are bad?**
A low silhouette score means the clusters aren't well-separated in the standardized feature space — which is true and worth saying plainly rather than hiding. But cluster quality for a business use case isn't the same question as geometric separation: even with overlapping PCA projections, the three clusters have sharply different real-world profiles (the AE-Prone cluster has a 99.8% any-AE rate and 29.8% dropout vs. ~8% dropout in the other two), which is what actually matters for a monitoring-prioritization decision. Patient phenotypes in general tend to sit on a continuum rather than in tight natural clusters, so a modest silhouette score is the expected, honest result here, not a red flag to paper over.

**3. Walk me through your feature selection and preprocessing for clustering.**
Features: age, BMI, baseline systolic/diastolic BP, baseline HbA1c, baseline LDL, baseline eGFR, primary outcome score, and each patient's worst adverse-event severity encountered (mapped None/Mild/Moderate/Severe → 0-3). I deliberately excluded treatment arm from the feature set — the goal was patient phenotypes that cut across treatment allocation, not a proxy for "which arm were you in" — though I do report arm composition per cluster during profiling. All features are standardized with `StandardScaler` before K-Means, since it's a distance-based algorithm and the raw features are on very different scales (age in years vs. outcome score 0-100).

**4. How did you turn three numbered clusters into business-meaningful labels without just eyeballing them?**
I used a ranked, rule-based approach rather than manual judgment calls (`label_all_clusters` in `patient_clustering.py`): any cluster with an elevated severe-AE rate is labeled "AE-Prone" first, since safety takes priority over efficacy in the labeling logic; among the remaining clusters, the highest mean outcome score becomes "High Responders" and the lowest becomes "Non-Responders." This is deterministic and reproducible — rerunning with different data (as happened when I scaled the dataset to 2,000 patients) relabels consistently rather than requiring a human to reinterpret the clusters each time. At the smaller 500-patient scale, k=4 produced an extra middle "Stable/Average Responders" bucket between High and Non-Responders; at 2,000 patients k=3 collapsed that distinction away, which the labeling function handled without any code changes.

**5. What would you do differently if a stakeholder said "these clusters don't feel actionable"?**
I'd first check whether the objection is about label naming or about whether the underlying groups are real — the AE-Prone cluster containing 161 of the trial's 162 severe adverse events (99.4%) is a real, actionable pattern regardless of what we call it. If the objection is about actionability specifically, I'd explore supervised segmentation instead (e.g. a decision tree predicting "will this patient become AE-Prone" from baseline covariates alone, before any post-baseline data exists) so the output is a rule clinicians can apply prospectively at enrollment, rather than a retrospective grouping that requires trial data to already exist.
